3.1 Physical Observation

Can We See Deformation?

The physical limits of direct human observation in engineering structures

The Engineering Observation

  • When a 500-ton train crosses a steel bridge, or when an airliner lands, do the steel girders visibly stretch?
  • A $1000\text{ mm}$ structural steel girder under a heavy stress of $150\text{ MPa}$ elongates by only $0.75\text{ mm}$ ($0.075\%$).
  • Human vision cannot detect whether a structural member is safely loaded at $20\%$ or on the brink of fatigue yield at $95\%$.
  • Core Problem: How do we convert an invisible mechanical displacement into an electrical signal?

Elastic Elongation Governing Law

$$\Delta L = \frac{F \cdot L_0}{A \cdot E}$$

For structural metals, elastic deflections $\Delta L$ range between $10^{-6}\text{ m}$ (1 micrometer) and $10^{-3}\text{ m}$ (1 millimeter).

3.1 Fundamentals

Understanding Strain ($\epsilon = \Delta L / L_0$)

Dimensionless deformation metric and the microstrain ($\mu\epsilon$) scaling

Definition & Mathematical Formulation

$$\epsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L_0} = \frac{L_{\text{final}} - L_0}{L_0}$$
  • Dimensionless Quantity: Ratio of length to length ($[\text{m}] / [\text{m}]$).
  • The Microstrain ($\mu\epsilon$): Because elastic strains are minute, engineers use: $$1\,\mu\epsilon = 10^{-6}\text{ m/m} = 0.0001\%$$
  • Yield Limit: Structural mild steel yields around $1200\text{ to }1500\,\mu\epsilon$.

Immediate Numerical Check

A $100.0\text{ mm}$ steel rod elongates by $0.10\text{ mm}$ during a tensile machine test. What is the indicated strain?

$$\epsilon = \frac{0.10\text{ mm}}{100.0\text{ mm}} = 0.0010 = \mathbf{1000\,\mu\epsilon}$$

A $1000\,\mu\epsilon$ strain corresponds to an internal tensile stress of $\sigma = E \cdot \epsilon = 200\text{ GPa} \times 0.001 = 200\text{ MPa}$.

3.1 Physics of Transduction

What Happens to Electrical Resistance?

The physical bridge linking mechanical elasticity to solid-state conduction

Governing Resistance Law

$$R = \rho \frac{L}{A}$$
  • Conductor Lengthening ($L \uparrow$): Axial tension increases path length; electrons collide with more vibrating crystal ions.
  • Lateral Thinning ($A \downarrow$): Poisson contraction narrows the cross-section, constricting electron flow.
  • Piezoresistive Lattice Shift ($\rho \pm$): Interatomic lattice spacing deformation alters conduction band electron mobility.

The Transduction Core

Mechanical Deformation $\implies$ Resistance Shift:
By bonding an ultra-thin conductive foil directly onto a mechanical specimen, surface strain $\epsilon$ is converted directly into a measurable change in resistance $\Delta R$.

Core Takeaway:

Tension stretches and narrows the wire $\implies \Delta R > 0$.
Compression shortens and thickens the wire $\implies \Delta R < 0$.

3.1 Transducer Anatomy

What Is a Strain Gauge?

Formal definition, operating principle, and structural components

"An electrical resistance strain gauge is a passive electromechanical sensor that measures surface mechanical strain by transducing dimensional and piezoresistive deformations into proportional variations in electrical resistance."
  • Metallic Foil Grid: Serpentine etched Constantan ($55\%\text{ Cu}, 45\%\text{ Ni}$) or Karma alloy foil ($3–5\,\mu\text{m}$ thick) maximizing longitudinal length while canceling transverse strain.
  • Polyimide Backing Matrix: Tough insulating film ($25\,\mu\text{m}$ thick) electrically isolating the grid from the metal beam.
  • Adhesive Layer: High-shear cyanoacrylate or epoxy transferring surface deformation into the grid via pure shear stress.
  • Solder Terminals: Reinforced copper tabs for lead-wire connection.

Standard Industrial Ratings

Nominal Resistance ($R_0$) $120\,\Omega$ or $350\,\Omega$
Nominal Gauge Factor ($GF$) $2.00\text{ to }2.15$
Max Elastic Strain Range $\pm 20,000\,\mu\epsilon$ ($2\%$)

3.1 Analytical Derivation

Derivation of Gauge Factor (GF)

First-principles breakdown from logarithmic differentiation of Ohm's resistance law

Step-by-Step Mathematical Proof

  1. Conductor resistance: $R = \rho \frac{L}{A}$
  2. Take natural logarithms: $\ln R = \ln \rho + \ln L - \ln A$
  3. Differentiate completely: $\frac{dR}{R} = \frac{d\rho}{\rho} + \frac{dL}{L} - \frac{dA}{A}$
  4. For a circular wire ($A = \frac{\pi}{4} D^2$): $\frac{dA}{A} = 2 \frac{dD}{D}$
  5. By Poisson's ratio ($\nu = -\frac{dD/D}{dL/L}$): $\frac{dA}{A} = -2\nu \frac{dL}{L} = -2\nu \epsilon$
  6. Substitute back: $\frac{dR}{R} = \epsilon + 2\nu\epsilon + \frac{d\rho}{\rho} = [1 + 2\nu + \frac{d\rho/\rho}{\epsilon}]\epsilon$

The Canonical Gauge Factor Equation

$$GF = \frac{\Delta R / R_0}{\epsilon} = 1 + 2\nu + \frac{\Delta\rho / \rho_0}{\epsilon}$$
  • $1$ (Axial Elongation): Direct path lengthening term.
  • $+2\nu$ (Poisson Contraction): Lateral area necking term ($2 \times 0.3 = 0.6$).
  • $\frac{\Delta\rho/\rho}{\epsilon}$ (Piezoresistive Shift): Solid-state lattice resistivity shift ($+0.4$ in Constantan; $+100$ in Silicon).

3.1 Worked Example

Gauge Factor: Solved Numerical Problem

Quantitative calculation of fractional resistance shift on a structural beam

Problem Statement

A standard metallic foil strain gauge with nominal resistance $R_0 = 120.00\,\Omega$ and gauge factor $GF = 2.00$ is bonded to a structural steel girder. Under peak tensile load, the measured strain is $\epsilon = 0.00100$ ($1000\,\mu\epsilon$).

Determine:
1. Fractional change in resistance ($\Delta R / R_0$)
2. Absolute resistance change ($\Delta R$ in $\Omega$)
3. Final strained resistance ($R_{\text{strained}}$)

Analytical Solution

Step 1: Fractional Resistance Shift
$$\frac{\Delta R}{R_0} = GF \cdot \epsilon = 2.00 \times 0.00100 = \mathbf{0.00200 = 0.20\%}$$
Step 2: Absolute Resistance Shift
$$\Delta R = R_0 (GF \cdot \epsilon) = 120.00\,\Omega \times 0.002 = \mathbf{+0.240\,\Omega}$$
Step 3: Strained Resistance
$$R_{\text{strained}} = 120.000 + 0.240 = \mathbf{120.240\,\Omega}$$
Key Pedagogical Takeaway: A heavy structural strain produces only a $0.24\,\Omega$ shift! That is why specialized bridge circuits are mandatory.

3.2 Bonded Gauges

Bonded Strain Gauge Construction

Carrier matrix, adhesive shear lag mechanics, and cross-sensitivity suppression

Structural Architecture

  • Etched Foil Grid ($3–5\,\mu\text{m}$): Constantan alloy photo-etched with wide end turnaround loops to minimize transverse strain sensitivity ($< 1\%$).
  • Polyimide Carrier ($25\,\mu\text{m}$): High-dielectric backing providing electrical isolation up to $1000\text{ V}$ and high fatigue endurance.
  • Bonding Matrix: Cyanoacrylate (room temp) or heat-cured epoxy ($150^\circ\text{C}$) transferring strain via pure shear lag.
  • Solder Pads: Copper pads for wire stress relief.

Shear Lag Transmission Theory

Surface strain $\epsilon_s$ is not transmitted instantly. Shear stress $\tau(x)$ in the glue layer builds normal strain $\epsilon_g(x)$ in the foil over a characteristic transfer length:

$$\tau_{\text{adhesive}}(x) = G_{\text{glue}} \frac{u_{\text{substrate}}(x) - u_{\text{foil}}(x)}{t_{\text{glue}}}$$

Improperly cured or overly thick adhesive layers cause viscoelastic creep, hysteresis, and loss of high-frequency fidelity.

3.2 Unbonded Gauges

Unbonded Strain Gauge Construction

Suspended filament flexures, mechanical pre-tension, and zero adhesive creep

Mechanical Flexure Assembly

  • Filament Wires ($25\,\mu\text{m}$): High-strength platinum-tungsten or nickel-chrome resistance wire wound under pre-tension between insulated sapphire posts.
  • Movable Flexure Armature: Connects to an external force or pressure diaphragm.
  • Push-Pull Arrangement: As the armature deflects, wires in group A tighten ($+\Delta R$) while wires in group B relax ($-\Delta R$).
  • Zero Organic Glue: Because no glue layer exists in the load train, organic viscoelastic creep is completely eliminated!

Primary Transducer Applications

  • Biomedical Blood Pressure Transducers: Extreme long-term baseline stability without zero drift.
  • Oceanographic Depth Capsules: Unbonded bridge diaphragms measuring hydrostatic pressures up to $70\text{ MPa}$.
  • Flight Inertial Accelerometers: Seismic mass suspended by unbonded flexure wires with built-in fluid damping.

3.3.1 Ballast Circuit

Ballast Circuit: Voltage Divider Analysis

Series topology, transfer equation, and sensitivity maximization at $m = R_b/R_g = 1$

Circuit Transfer Function

A series ballast resistor $R_b$ is connected with the strain gauge $R_g$ across excitation $V_s$. The output voltage across the gauge is:

$$V_0 = V_s \frac{R_g}{R_b + R_g}$$

Defining ballast ratio $m = R_b / R_g$, the incremental change in output voltage due to strain $\Delta R$ is:

$$\Delta V_0 = V_s \frac{m}{(1+m)^2} \frac{\Delta R}{R_0} = V_s \cdot \eta(m) \cdot (GF \cdot \epsilon)$$

Sensitivity Maximization Proof

To find the maximum sensitivity factor $\eta(m) = \frac{m}{(1+m)^2}$, we differentiate with respect to $m$ and set to zero:

$$\frac{d\eta}{dm} = \frac{(1+m)^2 - 2m(1+m)}{(1+m)^4} = \frac{1 - m}{(1+m)^3} = 0$$

$$\implies m = \frac{R_b}{R_g} = 1.00 \quad \implies \quad \eta_{\max} = \frac{1}{(1+1)^2} = \mathbf{0.25}$$

Maximum sensitivity occurs when the ballast resistor exactly equals nominal gauge resistance.

3.3.1 Ballast Circuit

Ballast Circuit: The Fatal DC Offset Flaw

Why ballast circuits fail for static strain and excel in dynamic vibration

The 5.000 V Resting Pedestal Problem

  • At optimal sensitivity ($m=1$), the resting output voltage is: $$V_0 = \frac{V_s}{2} = 5.000\text{ V (for } V_s = 10\text{ V)}$$
  • Under a massive $1000\,\mu\epsilon$ strain, $\Delta V_0 = 10 \times 0.25 \times 0.002 = 5.0\text{ mV}$.
  • A $5\text{ mV}$ signal riding on a $5000\text{ mV}$ DC pedestal requires an impossible $0.001\%$ ADC baseline stability!
  • Result: Ballast circuits are totally unusable for static weighing or steady structural loads.

The AC Dynamic Vibration Solution

By placing a series capacitor $C_b$ before the amplifier, the $5.0\text{ V}$ DC pedestal is completely blocked, passing only dynamic AC vibrations:

$$V_{\text{ac}}(t) = V_s \frac{m}{(1+m)^2} [GF \cdot \epsilon(t)], \quad f_{\text{cutoff}} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_{\text{in}} C_b}$$

Widely used in high-speed turbine blade dynamic vibration monitoring where DC baseline is irrelevant.

3.3.2 Bridge Circuits

The Wheatstone Bridge: Zero DC Offset

Differential diamond topology establishing a true zero-volt resting baseline

The Differential Diamond

Four resistive arms arranged in a closed loop. Excitation voltage $V_{EX}$ is applied across nodes A and C; differential output $V_0$ is measured between nodes B and D:

$$V_0 = V_B - V_D = V_{EX} \left( \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} - \frac{R_4}{R_3 + R_4} \right)$$
Bridge Null Balance Condition ($V_0 = 0.000\text{ V}$):
$$\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{R_4}{R_3} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad R_1 \cdot R_3 = R_2 \cdot R_4$$

Why It Overcomes the Ballast Circuit

  • Zero Resting Voltage: When initial arm resistances match, $V_0 = 0.000\text{ V}$.
  • Common-Mode Noise Rejection: Power supply noise affects both divider legs equally, subtracting to zero.
  • Full Dynamic Range: High-gain instrumentation amplifiers can amplify the microvolt strain signal directly without saturating from a DC pedestal.

3.3.2 Bridge Circuits

Quarter Bridge ($1\times$ Configuration)

Single active gauge with completion resistors, small-strain approximation & non-linearity

Transfer Function Derivation

Arm 1 has the active gauge ($R_1 = R_0 + \Delta R$); arms 2, 3, 4 are fixed resistors ($R_0$):

$$V_0 = V_{EX} \left( \frac{R_0 + \Delta R}{2R_0 + \Delta R} - \frac{1}{2} \right) = V_{EX} \left( \frac{\Delta R / R_0}{4 + 2(\Delta R / R_0)} \right)$$

Small-Strain Approximation: Since $\Delta R / R_0 \ll 1$, we drop $2(\Delta R / R_0)$ in the denominator:

$$V_0 \approx \frac{V_{EX}}{4} \frac{\Delta R}{R_0} = \mathbf{\frac{V_{EX}}{4} (GF \cdot \epsilon)}$$

Intrinsic Non-Linearity Error

The neglected term causes fractional non-linearity:

$$\text{Non-Linearity Error} \approx \frac{1}{2} \frac{\Delta R}{R_0} = \frac{1}{2} GF \cdot \epsilon$$

At $\epsilon = 1000\,\mu\epsilon$ ($GF=2$): Error is $\approx 0.10\%$.
At $\epsilon = 10,000\,\mu\epsilon$ (composites/plastics): Error rises to $1.0\%$, requiring software compensation.

3.3.2 Bridge Circuits

Half Bridge ($2\times$ Bending Configuration)

Two active complementary gauges: Doubled sensitivity, perfect linearity & thermal cancellation

The Mathematical Miracle of Push-Pull

On opposite surfaces of a cantilever beam in pure bending: $R_1 = R_0 + \Delta R$ (tension) and $R_2 = R_0 - \Delta R$ (compression):

$$V_0 = V_{EX} \left( \frac{R_0 + \Delta R}{(R_0 + \Delta R) + (R_0 - \Delta R)} - \frac{1}{2} \right)$$

Notice the denominator: $(R_0 + \Delta R) + (R_0 - \Delta R) = \mathbf{2R_0}$! The $\Delta R$ terms cancel identically!

$$V_0 = \frac{V_{EX}}{2} \frac{\Delta R}{R_0} = \mathbf{\frac{V_{EX}}{2} (GF \cdot \epsilon)}$$

Three Massive Advantages

  1. $2\times$ Sensitivity Multiplier: Exactly twice the voltage output of a quarter bridge.
  2. Zero Non-Linearity Error: Because the denominator sum is strictly constant, the transfer function is mathematically linear!
  3. Automatic Thermal Cancellation: Ambient temperature shifts affect both gauges equally ($\Delta R_T$), canceling completely in adjacent bridge arms!

3.3.2 Bridge Circuits

Full Bridge ($4\times$ Bending Configuration)

Four active gauges: Maximum sensitivity, highest SNR, and complete lead-wire immunity

Four-Arm Differential Summation

Two gauges on top in tension ($R_1 = R_3 = R_0 + \Delta R$) and two on bottom in compression ($R_2 = R_4 = R_0 - \Delta R$):

$$V_0 = V_{EX} \left( \frac{R_0 + \Delta R}{2R_0} - \frac{R_0 - \Delta R}{2R_0} \right) = V_{EX} \frac{2\Delta R}{2R_0}$$
$$V_0 = V_{EX} \frac{\Delta R}{R_0} = \mathbf{V_{EX} \cdot GF \cdot \epsilon}$$

$$S_{\text{full}} = 4 \times S_{\text{quarter}} = 2 \times S_{\text{half}}$$

The Gold Standard in Transducers

  • $4\times$ Sensitivity Gain: Quadruples millivolt output, maximizing Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR).
  • Full Thermal Rejection: Complete 4-arm symmetry cancels ambient thermal expansion.
  • Rejection of Spurious Loads: Cancels axial tension and torsional twisting while responding purely to bending moments.
  • Industrial Use: Standard in precision electronic weighing scales, dynamometers, and materials test frames.

3.3.3 Constant Current

4-Wire Kelvin Constant Current Circuit

Zero lead-wire attenuation and strictly linear transfer characteristics

4-Wire Kelvin Operating Principle

  • Current Loop (Leads 1 & 4): A precision current source injects an ultrastable current $I_0$ through the gauge.
  • Voltage Sense Loop (Leads 2 & 3): High-input-impedance amplifier ($R_{\text{in}} > 10\text{ G}\Omega$) reads voltage directly at gauge terminals.
  • Zero Voltage Drop in Sense Wires: Because $I_{\text{sense}} \approx 0$, the $I \cdot R_{\text{lead}}$ error is mathematically zero, regardless of cable length!

Strict Linearity Equation

Total gauge voltage is $V(t) = I_0(R_0 + \Delta R) = I_0 R_0 + I_0 \Delta R$. Subtracting the baseline $I_0 R_0$ gives:

$$\Delta V_0 = I_0 \cdot \Delta R = I_0 \cdot R_0 (GF \cdot \epsilon)$$
Key Advantage: Notice there is NO denominator term! Perfect mathematical linearity across the entire measurement range.

PART D: TEMPERATURE COMPENSATION

Understanding and neutralizing the two primary thermal error mechanisms

The Thermal Trap

An unloaded steel girder in sunlight heats up by $25^\circ\text{C}$. The gauge indicates hundreds of microstrain—false strain! If uncompensated, engineers mistake thermal expansion for dangerous mechanical overload.

Two Prescribed Solutions

  • 3.4.1 Adjacent Arm Balancing: Dummy gauge on unstressed matched coupon subtracts thermal shift algebraically.
  • 3.4.2 Self-Temperature Compensation (STC): Metallurgically tailoring alloy TCR to cancel substrate expansion directly.

3.4 Temperature Compensation

The Physics of Thermal Apparent Strain

Two distinct physical origins: Conductor resistivity change vs differential expansion

The Two Physical Mechanisms

  • 1. Thermal Coefficient of Resistance (TCR, $\beta$): The alloy's intrinsic electrical resistivity shifts with temperature: $$\left(\frac{\Delta R}{R_0}\right)_{\text{resistivity}} = \beta \cdot \Delta T$$
  • 2. Differential Thermal Expansion ($(\alpha_s - \alpha_g)$): Substrate expands at rate $\alpha_s$; gauge foil expands at rate $\alpha_g$. Substrate physically stretches the bonded foil: $$\epsilon_{\text{thermal}} = (\alpha_s - \alpha_g) \Delta T$$

The Apparent Strain Equation

$$\epsilon_{\text{app}} = \left[ \frac{\beta}{GF} + (\alpha_s - \alpha_g) \right] \Delta T$$

Alarming Scale: On an aluminum beam ($\alpha_s = 23\text{ ppm}/^\circ\text{C}$), a $25^\circ\text{C}$ temperature rise in an uncompensated gauge produces over $1000\,\mu\epsilon$ of fake apparent strain!

3.4.1 Adjacent Arm Balancing

Adjacent Arm Balancing (Dummy Gauge)

Using a matched dummy gauge on an unstressed specimen for algebraic cancellation

Physical Arrangement

  • Active Gauge ($R_1$): Bonded to the loaded mechanical member. Senses mechanical strain $\epsilon_{\text{mech}}$ plus ambient thermal drift $\Delta T$: $$\Delta R_1 = \Delta R_{\text{mech}} + \Delta R_T$$
  • Dummy Gauge ($R_2$): Bonded to an identical, unstressed piece of the same metal in immediate thermal contact. Senses only temperature: $$\Delta R_2 = \Delta R_T$$

Wheatstone Subtraction Proof

Connected into adjacent bridge arms ($R_1$ and $R_2$), the bridge output obeys:

$$V_0 \approx \frac{V_{EX}}{4} \left( \frac{\Delta R_1 - \Delta R_2}{R_0} \right)$$
$$\Delta R_1 - \Delta R_2 = (\Delta R_{\text{mech}} + \Delta R_T) - \Delta R_T = \mathbf{\Delta R_{\text{mech}}}$$

Thermal resistance shifts cancel out algebraically, leaving only the pure mechanical strain signal!

3.4.2 Self-Temperature Compensation

Self-Temperature Compensation (STC)

Metallurgical alloy tailoring to neutralize substrate thermal expansion

The Metallurgical Principle

What if there is no physical space for a dummy block? Metallurgists heat-treat and cold-work Constantan or Karma foil so its TCR $\beta$ is intentionally negative:

$$\beta = -GF \cdot (\alpha_s - \alpha_g)$$

The negative resistivity change cancels the positive mechanical elongation of the substrate, keeping $\epsilon_{\text{app}} \approx 0$ over $-20^\circ\text{C to }+150^\circ\text{C}$.

Standard Industrial STC Ratings

  • STC-13: Matched to mild steel & carbon steels ($\alpha_s \approx 11–13\,\text{ppm}/^\circ\text{C}$).
  • STC-23: Matched to 2024 / 7075 aluminum alloys ($\alpha_s \approx 23\,\text{ppm}/^\circ\text{C}$).
  • STC-06: Matched to titanium & stainless steel ($\alpha_s \approx 6\,\text{ppm}/^\circ\text{C}$).
Critical Warning: STC gauges work ONLY on their matched substrate material. Mounting an STC-13 gauge on aluminum creates huge errors!

3.4 Worked Example

Temperature Compensation: Solved Problem

Quantifying thermal drift vs mechanical strain on an aluminum aerospace beam

Problem Statement

An uncompensated gauge ($GF=2.0$, $\beta = +70\text{ ppm}/^\circ\text{C}$, $\alpha_g = 15\text{ ppm}/^\circ\text{C}$) is mounted on an aluminum beam ($\alpha_s = 23\text{ ppm}/^\circ\text{C}$, $E = 70\text{ GPa}$). Calibrated at $20^\circ\text{C}$, it operates in desert heat at $45^\circ\text{C}$ ($\Delta T = +25^\circ\text{C}$).

Determine:
1. Fractional resistance shift from temperature ($\Delta R / R_0$)
2. Thermal apparent strain ($\epsilon_{\text{app}}$ in $\mu\epsilon$)
3. Equivalent false stress ($\sigma_{\text{false}}$ in $\text{MPa}$)
4. Compare with an STC-23 matched gauge

Analytical Solution

Step 1: Fractional Resistance Shift
$$\frac{\Delta R}{R_0} = [70 + 2.0(23 - 15)] \times 10^{-6} \times 25 = \mathbf{2150\text{ ppm}}$$
Step 2: Thermal Apparent Strain
$$\epsilon_{\text{app}} = \frac{2150 \times 10^{-6}}{2.0} = \mathbf{+1075\,\mu\epsilon}$$
Step 3: False Stress in Aluminum ($E=70\text{ GPa}$)
$$\sigma_{\text{false}} = 70\text{ GPa} \times 1075 \times 10^{-6} = \mathbf{75.25\text{ MPa}!}$$
Step 4: STC-23 Gauge Performance: Apparent strain drops to $< \pm 15\,\mu\epsilon$ ($\sigma_{\text{false}} < 1.05\text{ MPa}$), a $98.6\%$ error reduction!

Solved Problem: Cantilever Load Cell Design

Step-by-step mechanical-to-electrical calculation for an industrial weigh cell

Given Engineering Parameters

  • Cantilever Beam: $b = 25\text{ mm}$, $h = 10\text{ mm}$, $L = 200\text{ mm}$
  • Steel Modulus: $E = 200\text{ GPa}$, Applied Tip Force: $F = 500\text{ N}$
  • Bridge: Full Bridge ($GF = 2.10$, $R_0 = 120\,\Omega$, $V_{EX} = 10.0\text{ V}$)
1. Bending Moment & Stress: $$M = F \cdot L = 500 \times 0.20 = 100\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$ $$Z = \frac{b h^2}{6} = \frac{0.025 \times 0.010^2}{6} = 4.167 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^3$$ $$\sigma = \frac{M}{Z} = \frac{100}{4.167 \times 10^{-7}} = \mathbf{240\text{ MPa}}$$

Electrical Output & Gain Calculation

2. Surface Mechanical Strain:
$$\epsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E} = \frac{240 \times 10^6}{200 \times 10^9} = \mathbf{1200\,\mu\epsilon}$$
3. Full Bridge Differential Output Voltage ($4\times$):
$$V_0 = V_{EX} \cdot GF \cdot \epsilon = 10.0 \times 2.10 \times 0.00120 = \mathbf{25.20\text{ mV}}$$
4. Required Instrumentation Amplifier Gain:
$$G_{\text{amp}} = \frac{V_{\text{ADC, FS}}}{V_{0, \max}} = \frac{5.000\text{ V}}{0.0252\text{ V}} \approx \mathbf{198.4 \approx 200\times}$$

University Exam Focus & High-Yield Questions

Frequent question patterns from Pokhara University and IOE examinations

Theoretical Exam Questions

  1. Derivation (5–8 Marks): Derive the expression for Gauge Factor ($GF = 1 + 2\nu + \frac{\Delta\rho/\rho}{\epsilon}$) from fundamentals and explain each term's physical meaning.
  2. Bridge Comparison (6 Marks): Compare Quarter, Half, and Full bridge configurations with neat sketches, transfer equations, and linearity proofs.
  3. Temperature Drift (5 Marks): Explain thermal apparent strain and show how an adjacent arm dummy gauge cancels thermal drift with algebraic derivation.
  4. STC Principle (4 Marks): Explain the metallurgical basis of Self-Temperature Compensation and state what happens if an STC-13 gauge is mounted on aluminum.

Numerical Exam Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Unit Traps: Forgetting that microstrain ($\mu\epsilon$) has a factor of $10^{-6}$. $800\,\mu\epsilon = 0.000800$.
  • Bridge Multipliers: Mixing up the 1/4 factor for Quarter bridge with the 1/2 factor for Half bridge and 1.0 factor for Full bridge.
  • Modulus Units: Converting $\text{GPa}$ to $\text{Pa}$ ($1\text{ GPa} = 10^9\text{ Pa}$) and dimensions from $\text{mm}$ to $\text{m}$.

Unit III Master Reference

Master Formula Reference Sheet

High-yield mathematical synthesis for engineering practice and examinations

1. Gauge Factor & Resistance

$$GF = 1 + 2\nu + \frac{\Delta\rho / \rho_0}{\epsilon}$$ $$\Delta R = R_0 \cdot GF \cdot \epsilon$$

2. Ballast Circuit ($m = R_b/R_g$)

$$\Delta V_0 = V_s \frac{m}{(1+m)^2} \frac{\Delta R}{R_0}$$ $$\eta_{\max} = 0.25 \quad \text{at } m = 1.0$$

3. Wheatstone Bridge Topologies

$$\text{Quarter: } V_0 \approx \frac{V_{EX}}{4} GF \epsilon$$ $$\text{Half: } V_0 = \frac{V_{EX}}{2} GF \epsilon$$ $$\text{Full: } V_0 = V_{EX} \cdot GF \cdot \epsilon$$

4. Constant Current 4-Wire

$$\Delta V_0 = I_0 \cdot R_0 \cdot GF \cdot \epsilon$$ $$\text{Zero lead-wire attenuation}$$

5. Apparent Thermal Strain

$$\epsilon_{\text{app}} = \left[\frac{\beta}{GF} + (\alpha_s - \alpha_g)\right]\Delta T$$ $$\text{STC Condition: } \beta = -GF(\alpha_s - \alpha_g)$$

6. Beam Bending Hooke's Law

$$\sigma = \frac{M \cdot y}{I} = \frac{M}{Z}, \quad \epsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E}$$ $$Z_{\text{rect}} = \frac{b h^2}{6}$$
